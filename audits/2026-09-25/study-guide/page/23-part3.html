Part 3 Splitting the data without cheating

A test score means something only if the model never saw the test examples, or near-copies of them. This corpus is full of near-copies: strains that are the same crystal turned around, two functionals computed at one geometry, folders that are byte copies of each other. So runs are grouped into units that always travel together, and every split assigns units, never single runs.

Orbits

The strain atlas strains a cubic crystal. Swapping two axes, or flipping an axis's sign, maps a strain onto an equivalent one: the same crystal, turned. The code reduces each run's six strain components to a canonical form under the 48 signed axis permutations, and every run sharing that form, under both functionals, belongs to one orbit. The 2,680 strain-atlas runs form 296 orbits. A random split would put one copy of a crystal in training and its turned twin in the test, and the test would measure memory.

The co-split rules

  • Byte-identical copies count once.
  • The cheap and accurate runs of one geometry always land in the same split.
  • The strain atlas and the supercell campaign share one fold map for the 120 shear values they have in common.
  • The alloy splits by configuration, each configuration taking its satellite runs along.
  • Every reported number is labeled interpolation (a held-out point between training points) or extrapolation (outside them).

Interpolation and extrapolation

Along a sweep, neighboring runs are nearly identical. Holding out a level that sits between two training levels tests interpolation, which a straight line between the neighbors often does almost perfectly. Holding out everything beyond the training range tests extrapolation. The perovskite split adds two extrapolation holdouts: every run with a lattice factor of 0.8, and separately every run with a factor of 1.2.

Found The perovskite split misses a symmetry

The perovskite split makes every run its own unit. But the cell's five atoms sit at the corner, the body center and the three edge midpoints, positions that swapping or reversing the cell's axes leaves unchanged. Reversing one cell edge turns the two angles touching it into their supplements, and 0.8 × 90° = 72° is the supplement of 1.2 × 90° = 108°. Many runs are therefore the same crystal written in a different cell.

Checked on 2 October with my own script. The 125 angle runs are only 14 distinct crystals. Every one of the 25 fold-0 angle runs has a same-crystal run in folds 1–4, and in each "extrapolation" holdout, 57 of the 61 runs are crystals that also sit among the runs not held out. On the fields themselves, pairs predicted to match do, to 2–6e-7 after the axis change (as stored they differ by 0.11–0.30). A genuinely different crystal stays at 8.2e-2 under all 48 axis changes.

Three smaller in-sample problems surfaced in the same checks. None flips a verdict, because each affected model lost even with the advantage.

  • The manifold decoder is compared with leave-one-level-out floors on 423 levels, but 337 of those levels are its own training data.
  • The Galerkin transformer's interpolation comparison scores it in-sample on 24 of 27 levels.
  • The two-input network's fixed bases are fit on folds 1–4, so the validation fold sits inside its own components. Its verdict against its twin is unaffected.
